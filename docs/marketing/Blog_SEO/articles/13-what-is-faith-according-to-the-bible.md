# What Is Faith According to the Bible?

Ask ten people to define faith and you will get answers ranging from "believing without evidence" to "staying positive" to "just trusting the universe." None of these is what the Bible means.

Scripture treats faith as a specific thing with a specific object — trust placed in a God who has acted and spoken, evidenced by a life that moves accordingly. It is neither blind nor a substitute for thinking, and it is never presented as a force that makes things happen by the strength of your conviction.

Here is how the Bible defines faith, what the Hebrew and Greek words carry, and the three mistakes that most distort it.

## Historical & Cultural Context: Faithfulness Before Belief

The Old Testament rarely discusses faith as an abstract inner state. Its main word, the Hebrew *emunah*, means firmness, steadiness, reliability — and it is used of God far more often than of people. God is "a God of truth" (*emunah*, Deuteronomy 32:4, KJV) — faithful, dependable, one whose promises hold.

So biblical faith begins as a description of God's character before it becomes a description of our response. Trusting God is reasonable because God is *emunah*. The ground floor is his reliability, not our sincerity.

The Old Testament's defining example is Abraham: "And he believed in the LORD; and he counted it to him for righteousness" (Genesis 15:6, KJV). Abraham had no Scriptures, no temple, no law — a promise and a God who made it. Paul later builds a whole argument on this verse (Romans 4), pointing out that Abraham was declared righteous by faith centuries before the Law existed, which means the Law was never the mechanism.

By the first century, the Greek *pistis* carried both "belief" and "faithfulness," and the New Testament uses that full range: what you are persuaded of, whom you trust, and how you then live.

## Section Breakdown: The Bible's Own Definition

**Hebrews 11:1 — the closest thing to a formal definition.** "Now faith is the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen" (KJV).

Two words are doing the work. *Hypostasis* ("substance") was used in commercial documents for a title deed or the assured basis of ownership — the present reality standing under a future claim. *Elenchos* ("evidence") is a legal term for proof that convicts or establishes a case. Neither word suggests guessing. Faith is described as the present grip on something real but not yet visible.

**Hebrews 11 then defines faith by biography, not theory.** Noah built before rain. Abraham left before he knew the address. Sarah conceived past the age. And verses 35-38 refuse to end the list at success stories: others were tortured, imprisoned, sawn asunder, and "received not the promise" (v. 39). Faith in Hebrews 11 is not a technique for getting outcomes. It is trust that persists whether or not the outcome arrives in this life.

**Faith has an object, and the object does the saving.** Scripture never commends faith in general. "Have faith in God" (Mark 11:22, KJV). And the New Testament names that object precisely: Christ, "who died for our sins according to the scriptures... and that he rose again the third day" (1 Corinthians 15:3-4, KJV). Saving faith is therefore never bare optimism about God in the abstract; it is repentant trust in a crucified and risen Saviour — what Paul summarised as "repentance toward God, and faith toward our Lord Jesus Christ" (Acts 20:21, KJV). Weak faith in a strong bridge gets you across; strong faith in a rotten one does not. The New Testament consistently locates the power in the one trusted, not the intensity of the trusting — which is why Jesus says mustard-seed faith is enough (Matthew 17:20).

**Faith comes from hearing, not from working it up.** "So then faith cometh by hearing, and hearing by the word of God" (Romans 10:17, KJV). In context Paul is describing the gospel preached to those who had not yet heard it (Romans 10:14-15), so faith arises as a response to God's revealed word, not as a mood a person manufactures. The same principle governs its growth: God's word — read and preached — rather than emotional effort, is the ordinary means.

**Faith and works are not rivals — but they are ordered.** Paul: "For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God: not of works, lest any man should boast" (Ephesians 2:8-9, KJV). James: "faith, if it hath not works, is dead, being alone" (James 2:17, KJV).

These are not in conflict. Paul is answering *how a person is justified before God* — by faith alone, never by works. James is answering *how you can tell whether a professed faith is real* — by whether anything follows. Paul rules works out as the root of salvation; James expects them as its fruit. Ephesians 2:10 holds both: saved not by works, but "created in Christ Jesus unto good works."

## Original Language Insights

**Hebrew *emunah*** — firmness, steadiness, reliability. Its root gives us "amen," which functions as an affirmation that something is firm and sure.

**Hebrew *batach*** — to trust, in the sense of leaning your weight on something. Proverbs 3:5 uses it: "Trust in the LORD with all thine heart."

**Greek *pistis*** — belief, trust, and faithfulness in one word. It covers content believed and loyalty rendered, which is why the New Testament can speak of "the faith" (a body of truth) and "your faith" (personal trust) with the same term.

**Greek *pisteuō eis*** — literally "believe *into*." John's characteristic phrase for saving faith: trust that moves toward and rests upon a person, not mere agreement about him.

## Common Misunderstandings

**Faith means believing without evidence.** Hebrews 11:1 calls faith *elenchos* — proof, evidence. The New Testament repeatedly appeals to testimony and witnesses (Luke 1:1-4; 1 Corinthians 15:3-8; John 20:30-31). Faith goes beyond sight, but it is not indifferent to truth.

**Faith is a force that makes things happen.** This inverts the direction. Scripture never puts the power in the believing; it puts it in the God believed. Treating faith as a currency that purchases outcomes — health, wealth, a specific answer — makes God a mechanism and the believer the real actor. Hebrews 11:35-39 explicitly honors those who trusted and did not receive.

**Doubt disqualifies you.** The father in Mark 9:24 says both "I believe" and "help thou mine unbelief" in one breath — and Jesus grants his request. Thomas doubted and was invited to touch. Doubt handled honestly, brought to God, is not the opposite of faith; settled refusal is.

**Faith is a feeling.** Feelings fluctuate with sleep, health, and circumstance. Biblical faith is trust that keeps acting when feelings are absent — Job trusting through loss, Abraham traveling without a map.

**If I just have enough faith, God must act.** Mustard-seed language rules this out. God is not obligated by the intensity of anyone's conviction; he acts according to his own wisdom and goodness. Faith trusts him *with* the outcome rather than dictating it.

## Practical Application

**Feed faith through Scripture, not through effort.** Romans 10:17 makes hearing God's word the ordinary source. If your faith feels thin, the first move is intake, not straining.

**Name the object.** When you say "I'm trusting God," finish the sentence: trusting him for what, based on what he has said? Vague faith collapses under pressure; faith attached to a specific promise does not.

**Bring your doubt to God rather than around him.** Mark 9:24 is a template prayer. Doubt discussed with God grows into faith far more often than doubt suppressed.

**Look for the follow-through.** James' test is practical: has anything in your calendar, budget, or relationships changed? Not as a way to earn anything — as a way to check that the root is alive.

**Expect obedience before clarity.** Abraham "went out, not knowing whither he went" (Hebrews 11:8, KJV). Faith frequently takes the first step before the full picture arrives.

## FAQ

**What is the biblical definition of faith?**
Hebrews 11:1: "faith is the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen" — a present, grounded confidence in what God has promised but you cannot yet see.

**Is faith blind belief?**
No. Hebrews uses the legal word *elenchos* (proof), and the New Testament repeatedly appeals to eyewitness testimony. Faith goes beyond sight, not against evidence.

**How much faith do I need?**
Jesus says faith like a mustard seed is enough (Matthew 17:20). The power lies in the object of faith, not its size.

**Do Paul and James contradict each other on faith and works?**
No. Paul explains how a person is justified — by faith alone. James explains how genuine faith is recognized — by the works that follow it.

**Can I have faith and doubt at the same time?**
Yes. "Lord, I believe; help thou mine unbelief" (Mark 9:24) is a prayer Jesus honored.

**How do I grow in faith?**
Primarily through Scripture — "faith cometh by hearing, and hearing by the word of God" (Romans 10:17) — alongside prayer, obedience, and the testimony of other believers.

**Does having enough faith guarantee healing or prosperity?**
No. Hebrews 11:35-39 commends believers who trusted God and suffered without receiving the promise in this life.

## Summary: Key Takeaways

- Biblical faith starts with God's reliability (*emunah*), not human sincerity.
- Hebrews 11:1 uses commercial and legal terms — title deed and proof — not language of guesswork.
- Faith always has an object — for saving faith, Christ crucified and risen; its power lies in the one trusted, not the intensity of the trusting.
- Faith comes by hearing God's word, so Scripture is the ordinary means of growth.
- Paul and James agree: works never save, and genuine faith always produces them.
- Doubt brought honestly to God is compatible with real faith; Hebrews 11 honors those who trusted without receiving.
