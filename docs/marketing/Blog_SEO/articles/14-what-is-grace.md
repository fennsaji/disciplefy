# What Is Grace? A Biblical Definition

Most Christians can recite a definition of grace — "unmerited favor" — and still find it hard to live as though it were true. The instinct to earn runs deep. We accept that we were saved by grace and then quietly assume we are kept by performance.

The Bible's picture of grace is larger than a definition. It is God's own disposition toward people who have no claim on him, expressed in a gift they could not obtain and cannot repay — and it does not stop working the moment a person is saved.

Here is what Scripture means by grace, where the word comes from, and the two opposite errors that most often distort it.

## Historical & Cultural Context: A Word Borrowed and Transformed

The Greek word behind "grace" is *charis*. In ordinary Greek usage it meant charm, favor, or a benefit given — and it operated inside a rigid patronage system. A patron granted favor; the recipient owed loyalty, service, and public honor in return. Favor created debt.

The New Testament takes that word and breaks the mechanism. In Paul's usage, *charis* is favor given to those who cannot reciprocate and are never asked to repay it — the gratitude, loyalty, and obedience it produces are grace's fruit, never its price. "And if by grace, then is it no more of works: otherwise grace is no more grace" (Romans 11:6, KJV). Paul is explicit that grace and earning are mutually exclusive categories — the moment you add a price, the thing is no longer grace.

The Old Testament background is equally important. The Hebrew *chen* means favor or graciousness — "But Noah found grace in the eyes of the LORD" (Genesis 6:8, KJV). Alongside it stands *chesed*, God's steadfast covenant love, often translated "mercy" or "lovingkindness." *Chesed* is loyal love that holds when the other party has broken the agreement, which is why Israel's history — repeated failure met with repeated faithfulness — is essentially a long demonstration of grace before the word "grace" is defined.

Grace is not a New Testament innovation. It is what God had been doing all along, named clearly at the cross.

## Section Breakdown: Four Things Grace Does

**Grace saves.** "For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God: not of works, lest any man should boast" (Ephesians 2:8-9, KJV). Every element is deliberate: the source is grace, the means is faith, the nature is gift, the excluded alternative is works, and the reason is that boasting must be impossible. Note that even the faith is not self-generated — Paul calls the whole arrangement God's gift.

**Grace justifies.** "Being justified freely by his grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus" (Romans 3:24, KJV). The word "freely" (*dōrean*) means as a gift, without cause in the recipient. Justification is a legal declaration: God declares the guilty righteous on the basis of Christ's work, not their improvement.

**Grace teaches and trains.** This is the half most often missed. "For the grace of God that bringeth salvation hath appeared to all men, teaching us that, denying ungodliness and worldly lusts, we should live soberly, righteously, and godly, in this present world" (Titus 2:11-12, KJV). Grace is not only pardon; it is an instructor. It does not merely forgive ungodliness — it teaches you to deny it.

**Grace sustains.** Paul asked three times for his thorn to be removed and received instead: "My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness" (2 Corinthians 12:9, KJV). Grace here is not a one-time entry ticket but ongoing supply — most evident where a person is weakest.

## Original Language Insights

**Greek *charis*** — favor, gift, kindness shown without obligation. It shares a root with *chara* (joy) and *charisma* (a gift of grace). Paul strips it of its patronage-debt framework.

**Greek *dōrean*** — freely, as a gift, without cause. Used in Romans 3:24 and elsewhere for something given for no reason found in the receiver.

**Hebrew *chen*** — favor, graciousness; the disposition of a superior toward one who has no claim.

**Hebrew *chesed*** — steadfast, covenant-keeping love; loyalty that persists after the other party has failed. The closest Old Testament equivalent to what the New Testament calls grace.

## Cross References

- **Ephesians 2:8-9** — saved by grace through faith, not of works.
- **Ephesians 2:10** — the sequel: "created in Christ Jesus unto good works." Works are the fruit, never the root.
- **Romans 3:23-24** — all have sinned; justified freely by his grace.
- **Romans 5:8** — "while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us" — grace directed at the actively undeserving.
- **2 Corinthians 5:21** — Christ made sin for us, that we might be made the righteousness of God in him: the exchange that makes grace both free and just.
- **Romans 6:1-2** — "Shall we continue in sin, that grace may abound? God forbid." Paul anticipates the abuse and rejects it.
- **Titus 2:11-12** — grace teaches godly living.
- **2 Corinthians 12:9** — grace sufficient in weakness.
- **John 1:16-17** — "grace for grace... grace and truth came by Jesus Christ."
- **Hebrews 4:16** — come boldly to the throne of grace to find help in time of need.

## Common Misunderstandings

**Grace means God overlooks sin.** It means the opposite. Grace is costly precisely because sin was not overlooked — it was judged, and Christ bore that judgment in the place of sinners. "The LORD hath laid on him the iniquity of us all" (Isaiah 53:6, KJV); "he hath made him to be sin for us, who knew no sin" (2 Corinthians 5:21, KJV). The cross is God satisfying his own justice, not waving sin through. Forgiveness that costs the forgiver nothing is indulgence, not grace.

**Grace is a licence to sin.** Paul raises this objection himself in Romans 6:1 and answers it with "God forbid" — the strongest negation available to him. His logic: a person united to Christ has died to sin, so continuing in it contradicts what grace accomplished. Titus 2 makes the same point positively: grace *teaches* us to deny ungodliness. Grace that leaves a life unchanged was misunderstood, not received.

**I was saved by grace but I'm kept by effort.** Paul confronted exactly this in Galatia: "Are ye so foolish? having begun in the Spirit, are ye now made perfect by the flesh?" (Galatians 3:3, KJV). The same grace that saves also sustains. Christian growth is grace-powered effort, not grace-plus-self-improvement.

**Grace makes obedience optional.** Grace changes obedience's motive, not its existence. Duty performed to secure God's acceptance becomes gratitude expressed because acceptance is already secure. Ephesians 2:10 puts good works immediately after "not of works" — as the purpose, not the price.

**Some sins are too big for grace.** Paul called himself the chief of sinners after persecuting the church (1 Timothy 1:15). David committed adultery and arranged a death. The New Testament's authorship and leadership are full of people whose sins would disqualify them under any earning system. That is the point of the word.

**Grace is only about salvation.** Scripture speaks of grace for weakness (2 Corinthians 12:9), grace for service (Romans 12:6), grace in speech (Colossians 4:6), and grace found at the throne "to help in time of need" (Hebrews 4:16). It is a daily supply, not a single transaction.

## Practical Application

**Notice where you're still paying.** When you sin, does your instinct run toward confession or toward a penance of self-punishment before you feel allowed to pray again? The second is an earning reflex, and it is exactly what grace removes.

**Let grace do its teaching work.** Titus 2 assigns grace an active role in your holiness. Fighting sin by white-knuckled willpower alone tends to collapse; fighting it as someone already accepted, empowered, and taught tends to last.

**Approach God boldly, especially when you've failed.** Hebrews 4:16 says come boldly *to find help in time of need* — the moment of failure is the intended use case, not the disqualifier.

**Extend the arithmetic outward.** Jesus' parable of the unforgiving servant (Matthew 18:21-35) turns on a man who received an unpayable pardon and then throttled a debtor over pocket change. Received grace that does not flow outward has probably not been understood.

**Stop measuring yourself against other Christians.** Comparison is an earning framework in disguise. Grace has no ranking table.

## FAQ

**What is the simplest biblical definition of grace?**
God's undeserved favor — kindness given freely to people who have no claim on it and cannot repay it.

**What is the difference between grace and mercy?**
Mercy withholds a deserved judgment; grace gives an undeserved gift. At the cross both happen at once.

**Is grace only in the New Testament?**
No. The Hebrew *chen* (favor) and *chesed* (steadfast covenant love) run through the Old Testament — Noah "found grace in the eyes of the LORD" in Genesis 6:8.

**Does grace mean I can keep sinning?**
No. Paul raises that exact question in Romans 6:1 and answers "God forbid." Titus 2:11-12 says grace teaches us to deny ungodliness.

**Can I lose God's grace by sinning too much?**
Grace is not a quantity you drain. 1 Timothy 1:15-16 has Paul, a former persecutor of the church, presenting himself as the example case for how far it reaches.

**What does "the grace of God is sufficient" mean?**
From 2 Corinthians 12:9 — God's supply is enough for the situation even when the situation isn't removed. Paul's thorn stayed; the grace was given instead.

**If I'm saved by grace, why do good works matter?**
Ephesians 2:10 answers it: we are saved *for* good works, not *by* them. Works are the evidence and purpose of grace, never its price.

## Summary: Key Takeaways

- Grace (*charis*) is favor given to those who cannot repay — Paul deliberately strips the word of its debt-creating patronage context.
- The Old Testament equivalents are *chen* (favor) and *chesed* (steadfast covenant love); grace is not a New Testament innovation.
- Grace saves (Ephesians 2:8-9), justifies (Romans 3:24), teaches godly living (Titus 2:11-12), and sustains in weakness (2 Corinthians 12:9).
- Grace does not overlook sin; it pays for it. The cost is what proves sin was taken seriously.
- Romans 6:1 rejects grace as a licence to sin; Galatians 3:3 rejects grace-then-effort as the growth model.
- Good works follow grace as fruit (Ephesians 2:10), never precede it as payment.
