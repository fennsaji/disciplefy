# What Is Repentance in the Bible?

Repentance has a public-relations problem. The word conjures street-corner signs and a tone of accusation, so many Christians quietly file it under "things preached at other people."

But repentance is the first word of Jesus' public ministry — "Repent ye, and believe the gospel" (Mark 1:15, KJV) — and it is not primarily about feeling bad. It is about changing direction. And it is not only the entrance to the Christian life; it is a rhythm within it.

Here is what Scripture means by repentance, how it differs from regret, and what it looks like in practice.

## Historical & Cultural Context: Two Words, One Turn

The Old Testament's main word is the Hebrew *shuv* — to turn, to return. It is a physical, directional word, used of a traveler turning around on a road. When prophets called Israel to repent, they were calling a people who had walked away to walk back: "Turn ye even to me with all your heart" (Joel 2:12, KJV).

That framing matters. Repentance in the Hebrew Bible is relational and covenantal — a return to a person you belong to, not merely regret over a rule you broke.

The Greek word in the New Testament is *metanoia*, from *meta* (change) and *nous* (mind). It denotes a change of mind that reorients the whole person — not a passing thought, but a settled reversal in how you see God, yourself, and your sin, which then changes direction.

Put together, the Bible's picture of repentance is a change of mind (*metanoia*) that produces a change of direction (*shuv*). Feelings often accompany it. They are not the substance of it.

The word carried weight in the first century because John the Baptist demanded it of everyone, including the religiously respectable — and demanded evidence: "Bring forth therefore fruits meet for repentance" (Matthew 3:8, KJV). When soldiers and tax collectors asked what that meant, he gave them specifics about their money and their power (Luke 3:10-14). Repentance was expected to show up in behavior.

## Section Breakdown: Four Elements

**A change of mind about sin.** Repentance begins with seeing sin as God sees it — not as a mistake, a personality trait, or a coping mechanism, but as an offense against God. David, after adultery and arranged murder, prays: "Against thee, thee only, have I sinned" (Psalm 51:4, KJV). He had clearly wronged Uriah and Bathsheba; his point is that the deepest wound was against God.

**Godly sorrow, distinguished from worldly sorrow.** "For godly sorrow worketh repentance to salvation not to be repented of: but the sorrow of the world worketh death" (2 Corinthians 7:10, KJV). Both kinds of sorrow hurt. Godly sorrow is grieved at having sinned against God and moves toward him. Worldly sorrow is grieved at consequences, exposure, or damaged self-image, and moves inward — toward self-pity, or away in shame.

Judas and Peter both failed Jesus in the same week — one betrayed him, the other denied him three times. Judas felt remorse and went out and hanged himself. Peter wept bitterly (Luke 22:62) and was restored (John 21:15-19). The difference was not the size of the failure or the depth of the feeling; it was the direction it moved them. And that direction was not finally something Peter supplied: Jesus had already told him, "I have prayed for thee, that thy faith fail not" (Luke 22:32, KJV).

**Confession.** "If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness" (1 John 1:9, KJV). The Greek *homologeō* means to say the same thing — agreeing with God about what the sin is, without minimizing, renaming, or blame-shifting. Note the word *just*: God forgives without setting justice aside because the penalty has already been borne — "the blood of Jesus Christ his Son cleanseth us from all sin" (1 John 1:7, KJV). Confession is not the payment; it is how the payment is received.

**Turning — with fruit.** Repentance is incomplete until direction changes. "Let the wicked forsake his way... and let him return unto the LORD" (Isaiah 55:7, KJV). Paul summarized his whole ministry as calling people to "repent and turn to God, and do works meet for repentance" (Acts 26:20, KJV). Zacchaeus is the model: he did not merely apologize for extortion, he repaid fourfold (Luke 19:8).

## Original Language Insights

**Hebrew *shuv*** — to turn back, return. Directional and relational; used of returning to a place or a person.

**Hebrew *nacham*** — to be sorry, to grieve, to be moved to compassion. Sometimes translated "repent," including in passages about God "repenting" (relenting) — which describes a change in dealings, not a confession of sin.

**Greek *metanoia*** — a change of mind that reorients the person. The New Testament's primary word for repentance.

**Greek *metamelomai*** — to feel regret or remorse. This is the word used of Judas in Matthew 27:3: regret without reorientation. The word is not always negative, though — Matthew 21:29 uses it of a son who changed his mind and obeyed — so context, not the word by itself, marks the difference.

**Greek *epistrephō*** — to turn around, convert. Frequently paired with *metanoeō* in Acts: change your mind *and* turn (Acts 3:19).

## Cross References

- **Mark 1:15** — Jesus' opening message: "Repent ye, and believe the gospel."
- **Acts 2:38** — Peter at Pentecost: "Repent, and be baptized every one of you" — baptism as the immediate public sign of repentance and faith, not the ground of forgiveness (Ephesians 2:8-9).
- **Acts 3:19** — "Repent ye therefore, and be converted, that your sins may be blotted out."
- **Luke 15:11-24** — the prodigal son: "I will arise and go to my father" — the clearest narrative picture of *shuv*.
- **Psalm 51** — David's repentance in full: confession, godly sorrow, a plea for a clean heart.
- **2 Corinthians 7:9-11** — godly vs. worldly sorrow, and the fruit godly sorrow produces.
- **1 John 1:8-9** — confession and cleansing for believers.
- **Luke 19:1-10** — Zacchaeus, repentance with restitution.
- **Revelation 2:5** — Christ calls a *church* to repent: "remember therefore from whence thou art fallen, and repent, and do the first works."
- **Romans 2:4** — "the goodness of God leadeth thee to repentance."

## Common Misunderstandings

**Repentance is feeling bad about sin.** Feelings often come with it, but 2 Corinthians 7:10 distinguishes two sorrows with opposite outcomes. You can feel terrible and never repent, and you can repent through numbness. The test is direction, not intensity.

**Repentance means never sinning again.** Repentance is a turn, and turns can be repeated. Luke 17:4 anticipates a brother sinning and repenting seven times in a day and instructs forgiveness each time. Falling again does not retroactively invalidate a genuine turn — though a pattern with no change at all is worth honest examination.

**Repentance is a work that earns forgiveness.** Romans 2:4 reverses the order: it is God's kindness that leads to repentance. Repentance is the empty hand opening, not the payment placed in it. Even the ability to repent is described as something God grants (Acts 11:18; 2 Timothy 2:25).

**Repentance is only for non-Christians.** Revelation 2-3 records the risen Christ calling five of seven churches to repent. 1 John 1:9 is written to believers. Martin Luther's first of the Ninety-Five Theses made the point sharply: the whole life of a believer is meant to be one of repentance.

**Confession alone is repentance.** Saying it is one element. Repentance includes the turn, and where harm was done, restitution where possible — the Zacchaeus pattern.

**Repentance is mostly about God's anger.** Romans 2:4 names God's goodness as the motive, and Luke 15 pictures the father running toward the returning son. Fear may start someone moving; grace is what the Bible says draws them.

## Practical Application

**Name it specifically.** "Forgive my sins" is vague enough to change nothing. "I lied to my manager on Tuesday to protect myself" is confessable, turnable, and correctable.

**Check the direction of your sorrow.** Ask: am I grieved that I sinned against God, or that I got caught, embarrassed, or inconvenienced? The first moves you toward God; the second moves you inward.

**Identify the turn.** Every genuine repentance has a concrete next step. What will you stop, start, avoid, or repair? If nothing changes on your calendar, in your habits, or in a relationship, the turn hasn't happened yet.

**Repair where you can.** Restitution isn't payment for forgiveness — it is evidence the turn is real. Return what was taken; correct what was misrepresented; apologize to whom you wronged.

**Make it a rhythm.** A weekly or daily examination — where did I go my own way today? — keeps repentance small and frequent rather than rare and overwhelming.

**Don't stall in shame.** Shame keeps you circling the failure; repentance moves you toward God. If you find yourself rehearsing guilt without going anywhere, that is worldly sorrow, and it "worketh death."

## FAQ

**What is the biblical definition of repentance?**
A change of mind about sin (Greek *metanoia*) that results in turning from it back to God (Hebrew *shuv*), evidenced by changed behavior.

**What is the difference between repentance and regret?**
Regret feels bad about consequences; repentance turns toward God and changes direction. 2 Corinthians 7:10 calls these worldly and godly sorrow — one leads to death, the other to life.

**Do I have to feel emotional to truly repent?**
No. Emotion often accompanies repentance but is not its substance. The measurable element is the turn, not the tears.

**Is repentance necessary for salvation?**
Yes. Jesus' first recorded message pairs it with faith: "Repent ye, and believe the gospel" (Mark 1:15). Repentance and faith are two aspects of one turning.

**Do Christians need to keep repenting?**
Yes. 1 John 1:9 is addressed to believers, and Revelation 2-3 records Christ calling churches to repent. Repentance is a lifelong rhythm. It restores fellowship with the Father; it does not re-earn a standing already settled in Christ — "there is therefore now no condemnation to them which are in Christ Jesus" (Romans 8:1, KJV).

**What if I keep committing the same sin?**
Luke 17:4 assumes repeated repentance and repeated forgiveness. Keep turning, and address the pattern practically — accountability, removing access, seeking help — rather than concluding you're beyond it.

**Does repentance earn God's forgiveness?**
No. Romans 2:4 says God's goodness leads us to repentance; forgiveness rests on Christ's work, and repentance is how it is received.

**What is the difference between confession and repentance?**
Confession is agreeing with God about the sin; repentance includes that agreement plus the turn away from it.

## Summary: Key Takeaways

- Repentance combines *metanoia* (a change of mind) with *shuv* (a change of direction) — thinking differently and then walking differently.
- Godly sorrow moves you toward God; worldly sorrow moves you inward and "worketh death" (2 Corinthians 7:10). Peter and Judas are the contrast.
- It includes confession — *homologeō*, saying the same thing God says about the sin.
- Genuine repentance produces fruit: a concrete turn, and restitution where harm was done (Luke 19:8).
- It is not a payment that earns forgiveness; God's kindness leads to it (Romans 2:4).
- Repentance is not only an entry point but a lifelong rhythm — Christ calls churches to repent (Revelation 2:5).
